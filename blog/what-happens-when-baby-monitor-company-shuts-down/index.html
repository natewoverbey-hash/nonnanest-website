What Happens to Your Baby Monitor When the Company Shuts Down?

A monitor that leans on a company's servers to work is only as reliable as that company. Here's what can go wrong if the lights go out — and how to choose one that keeps working regardless.

If a baby monitor depends on a company's cloud servers to function, and that company shuts down or stops supporting the product, the monitor can lose features or stop working altogether. It sounds dramatic, but it's a real risk with any device whose core functions run through the manufacturer's servers rather than on the device itself. The good news is that it's an avoidable risk once you know what to look for. Here's how cloud dependence works, what can happen when support ends, and how to choose a monitor that keeps doing its job no matter what.

What does it mean for a monitor to be "cloud-dependent"?

A cloud-dependent monitor sends your baby's video and data out of your home to the manufacturer's servers, does its processing there, and sends the result back to your phone through an app. That round trip is what enables features like remote viewing, stored video history, and long-term trend charts.

The catch is that the round trip only works as long as those servers are running and the app is maintained. The device in your nursery isn't fully self-sufficient — it's one end of a service that someone else has to keep switched on.

What can happen if the company goes away?

When a company that runs a cloud-dependent product shuts down, stops updating the app, or drops support for an older model, owners can run into problems like these:

  • Features stop working. Anything that relied on the servers — remote access, history, analytics — can simply go dark.
  • The app breaks over time. Phone operating systems update constantly. An app that's no longer maintained eventually stops keeping up and can fail to open or connect.
  • Support disappears. When there's no company left, there's no one to call, no replacement parts, and no fix coming.
  • Previously included features get gated. In some cases, a company under financial pressure moves features that used to be free behind a paywall before it winds down.

For most gadgets, that's an annoyance. For the device you rely on to check on your sleeping baby, it's the kind of thing worth avoiding before you ever buy.

How to tell how dependent a monitor is

You don't need to be technical to gauge this. A few plain questions do the job:

  • Does it need an app and an account to work at all? If the answer is yes, the company sits between you and your monitor every night.
  • Where does the video go? If it travels to the cloud, it depends on that cloud staying online.
  • Will it still function with the internet off? If turning off your WiFi turns off the monitor, that tells you how self-sufficient it really is.
  • Is there a monthly plan? A subscription is a sign that core value lives on someone else's servers.

What a self-contained monitor looks like

Some monitors are built to work entirely on their own. Instead of sending video to the cloud and back, they transmit directly from the camera to a dedicated parent unit over a closed, private signal — no account, no app, no company server in the middle. A common approach is FHSS (frequency-hopping spread spectrum), which keeps the connection between just two paired devices.

The practical upshot is durability. A monitor that does everything locally doesn't depend on a company keeping servers online to keep functioning. It works on day one and it works the same way years later, because nothing it needs lives somewhere you can't control. For a product parents lean on every night, that independence is a feature in its own right.
