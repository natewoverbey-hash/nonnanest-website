A Nurse’s Honest Take on Baby Monitors

Why we ended up wanting a wellness baby monitor for our own kids.

By Emily Boesner, RN, BSN

Editor’s note

Disclosure: The author is married to Nathan Overbey, founder of Nonnanest. She writes here as a parent and registered nurse sharing her personal experience. Her views are her own.

I’m a nurse. I’m also a mom of two. People assume that because of the nurse part, I had the whole baby-monitor thing figured out when we had our son. I really didn’t.

We bought a regular video and audio baby monitor, like most people do. It was fine. Until one night when it wasn’t.

Something felt off with him. And I kept looking at the monitor thinking, this is not telling me anything useful right now. I could see him. I could hear him. But I couldn’t actually tell what was going on with him, and it kept bothering me.

That night is honestly what got us looking at what else was out there. And what we found was that even the wellness baby monitors on the market weren’t measuring the one thing we would have wanted to know that night, which was whether his skin temperature was changing while he slept.

So that’s why I care about the word wellness. Not because it sounds nice on a box. Because it’s an honest word for what a baby monitor like this actually does. It’s not a doctor. It’s not a diagnosis. It just helps you notice things.

Hospitals don’t watch every number all the time

Something a lot of parents don’t realize is how monitoring actually works in a clinic.

I work in outpatient cardiac care. When a patient comes in, we take their temperature at the beginning. Regular thermometer, under the tongue. That’s it, unless there’s a specific reason to check again. If they’re having anesthesia, we’ll take another read. In the recovery room we might check under the tongue or under the arm, mostly to make sure they haven’t gotten too cold.

So no. We’re not watching every number every second. Nobody is. And we don’t need to.

I say all that because when we were picking a baby monitor for our own home, I wasn’t looking to turn our house into a mini hospital. That’s not what any home needs. I just wanted something quieter. A signal that something had shifted, so I could decide whether to go check on him.

Skin temperature isn’t a fever reading, but it tells you something real

This is probably the most important thing I want to explain, so bear with me.

When your pediatrician asks if your child has a fever, they’re asking about core body temperature. That’s what you get from an actual thermometer, under the tongue or in the ear. Skin temperature is different. It’s what’s happening at the surface of your skin, and it moves around based on the room, the pajamas, the blanket, all kinds of things.

But different doesn’t mean unrelated. Your skin is one of the ways your body regulates temperature. So when something’s going on internally, your skin usually reflects it. This is well documented. Normally your skin runs a couple of degrees cooler than your core. When your body is working harder to shed heat, that gap narrows and skin temperature goes up. A sustained rise from your child’s normal baseline can be one of the earlier signs that something is happening.

So no, skin temperature isn’t a diagnosis. But it’s a real signal, and it moves in ways that track with what’s actually going on inside.

That’s what I wanted. Not something telling me “your child has a fever.” Something telling me “hey, this shifted from their normal, maybe go check.”

On the normal nights I don’t need alerts. On the nights when something’s changing, I want to know sooner rather than later.

I didn’t want a medical device in my nursery

This might sound weird coming from a nurse.

But I really didn’t want something in my nursery that was trying to act medical. Here’s what I mean. When a product acts like it’s medical, you start treating it like it is. Every alert feels clinical. Every reading feels like something you’re supposed to interpret. And next thing you know, you’re up at 2 AM on WebMD. That’s not peace of mind. That’s the opposite of it.

A wellness device is honest about what it is. It gives you useful information. It doesn’t pretend to know more than it does. And it doesn’t tell you what to do with the information, because that’s my job. And my pediatrician’s. And honestly, that’s how it should be.

Peace of mind shows up on the hard nights

Most nights are quiet. You feed, you rock, you put them down, you go to bed. The baby monitor does its thing in the background and you barely think about it. That’s how it should be.

The nights that matter are the other ones. When your kid was a little off at dinner. When you just have a feeling. When one of them is already fighting something and you’re worried it’s going to get worse overnight.

That’s when the monitor earns its spot in our house. Not by drowning you in numbers on the easy nights. By being there on the hard ones.

Our number one job is keeping our kids safe. So anything that helps me do that a little better, without turning our home into a clinic, is something I want in the house.

Why contactless was the right call for us

Some baby monitors use a wearable, like a sock or an ankle band. Some families really like that. It just wasn’t what we wanted.

I liked the idea of a baby monitor that didn’t need to be on my kid at all. Nothing to slip, nothing to shift, nothing to remember to charge and put on him before bed. Just a small unit that sits up above the crib or bed and reads their skin temperature from overhead, and shows it on the display next to my bed. That felt right for us.

This isn’t just for babies

Something we don’t talk about enough. Kids grow out of cribs. They don’t grow out of needing us at night.

Our two are 4 and 7 now. They can tell me when they don’t feel good, at least when they’re awake. But the moments I worry about most are the ones where they’re asleep and something is changing and they can’t tell me anything. That’s when having a baby monitor that’s actually paying attention matters more, I think. Not less.

A wearable sock isn’t going on a 4-year-old. And my 7-year-old isn’t wearing anything to bed, either. So if you want any kind of overnight awareness past the baby stage, honestly a contactless baby monitor is really the only option I’ve found. Most parents don’t even realize that’s a category.

Our job to keep our kids safe doesn’t end when they move into a big-kid bed. It just looks different. Having a baby monitor that works across all the stages we’re in right now has been really helpful, and I didn’t expect to love it as much as I do.

When to call your pediatrician

If you notice your child’s skin temperature has shifted, and you go check on them and they seem warmer or cooler than usual, or fussy, or sleepy in a way that feels off, or just not themselves, trust yourself. Grab a real thermometer. And call your pediatrician if anything’s nagging at you.

No monitor replaces that call. Not this one, not any of them. All a good monitor can do is help you notice sooner. Which usually means you make the call sooner too. And that matters.
